Welcome. In 60 minutes we're going to answer three questions: (1) How does AI actually work under the hood? (2) How do I talk to it without getting fooled? (3) How do I make it use MY numbers, MY policies, MY documents? You'll run exercises live on your phones and laptops. Use Gemini, ChatGPT, or Claude — any of them.
Two accountants, same task: "explain why travel expense is up 40% this month." Left — lazy prompt gives a generic textbook answer. Right — an engineered prompt produces a specific, actionable memo. Same AI, 50x better output. That gap is what today is about.
AI is not Google. It does not "look up" answers. It predicts what words should come next based on patterns it learned from billions of documents. Think of it as an extremely well-read assistant who writes fresh content — not a filing cabinet.
Tokens are how AI counts. Roughly: 1 token ≈ 0.75 words, or 4 characters. Why care? Because tokens cost money and tokens fill up memory. Example: summarizing your firm's monthly expense report (~2,000 words) = ~2,700 tokens. Pennies per query, but it adds up at scale.
Before AI can "think" about words, it turns them into numbers — lists of numbers called embeddings or vectors. The word "accrual" becomes something like [0.8, 0.2, 0.5, 0.9, ...]. Why? Computers do math, not English. This math captures meaning.
In this number-space, similar meanings cluster together. "Late payment," "past due," and "overdue" sit right next to each other. "Depreciation," "amortization," and "useful life" form their own cluster. "Unicorn" is far, far away. This is how AI "understands" that two phrases mean the same thing even when the words are different.
Concrete payoff: imagine 10,000 customer transaction descriptions. Search for "refund" the old way — you only find rows containing the literal word "refund." AI-powered semantic search finds "refund" AND "money back" AND "credit issued" AND "returned item." That's the power of meaning-based search. Huge for audit, accounts payable, fraud detection.
Same word, different meanings. "Close the books" = finish the accounting period. "Close the door" = shut it. How does AI know which one? The "attention mechanism" — it looks at every other word in the sentence simultaneously and uses them to disambiguate. The word "books" tells attention: this is accounting, not doors.
When you ask AI about "depreciation on the delivery truck," the word "depreciation" pays attention to "delivery truck" to figure out we're talking about a physical asset (not, say, a reputation depreciation). This happens in parallel, for every word in your prompt, every time. It's why modern AI is so much better than chatbots from 10 years ago.
At its core, AI predicts the next token from a list of probabilities. "Revenue..." → increased (34%), decreased (28%), grew (15%). TEMPERATURE is a dial from 0 to 1: Low (0.1) = picks the most likely word, very predictable, great for journal entries and tax calcs. High (0.9) = picks less likely words, more creative, great for brainstorming a client thank-you note. Accounting default: keep it LOW.

Exercise 1: The Temperature Challenge

Open any AI chat. Run each prompt 3 times and watch what changes.

Prompt A: Write a one-sentence description of accounts receivable. Prompt B: Write a one-sentence description of accounts receivable. Be factual, concise, and use the same wording every time.
Discuss: Which prompt was more consistent? When does that matter (journal entry memo vs. LinkedIn post)?
AI doesn't know what it doesn't know. Ask it for the CFO of a small private company it's never heard of — it'll invent a name that "sounds like" a CFO name. Ask for a recent tax law change — it might invent a fake code section. This isn't malice; it's how the math works. The model predicts "plausible" text, and sometimes plausible ≠ true.
Three real cases. (1) Air Canada chatbot invented a refund policy — tribunal ruled the airline had to honor it. (2) Two NYC lawyers filed a brief with six AI-invented court cases; judge fined them and sanctioned them publicly. (3) An accountant used AI to research a tax position, AI cited a nonexistent IRS revenue ruling — the firm caught it before filing, but imagine if they hadn't. Confidence is not accuracy.
Six tactics to cut hallucinations dramatically: (1) GROUND IT — upload the actual source document, don't rely on memorized training data. (2) LOWER TEMPERATURE — 0.1 to 0.3 for factual work. (3) DEMAND CITATIONS — "quote the exact sentence you used, with page number." (4) ASK "WHAT WOULD MAKE YOU WRONG?" — forces the model to flag its uncertainty. (5) TWO-MODEL CHECK — ask Gemini AND Claude the same question; if they disagree, dig in. (6) GOLDEN RULE — a human verifies before anything gets signed, filed, or sent. You are still responsible.
Honest update: hallucinations were much worse two years ago. Today's flagship models — Claude Opus 4.7, Gemini 3.1, GPT-5 — hallucinate noticeably less, refuse more often when unsure, and cite sources better. On benchmarks like SimpleQA, modern models now correctly say "I don't know" at much higher rates. BUT: "better" ≠ "solved." In accounting, professional liability still lives with YOU. Verify every number, every citation, every client name.
For today's exercises you can use any of these three — Gemini (gemini.google.com), ChatGPT (chatgpt.com), or Claude (claude.ai). They're all free at the basic tier. They all behave similarly for the exercises we'll do. Pick whichever you already have, or try a new one.
One framework to remember: RCTFC. Role — who should the AI pretend to be? Context — what background info does it need? Task — the actual instruction. Format — how should the output look (email, table, bullet list)? Constraints — length, tone, boundaries. Memorize these five letters. This is 80% of good prompting.
Walk through a real accounting prompt using RCTFC. ROLE: senior bookkeeper. CONTEXT: monthly reconciliation shows a $340 unexplained difference between bank statement and cash ledger. TASK: draft 3 follow-up questions to email the office manager. FORMAT: numbered list with a one-line rationale each. CONSTRAINT: polite, professional, under 100 words. Notice how each piece eliminates ambiguity — the AI now has everything it needs.

Exercise 2: Build Your First RCTFC Prompt

You're an AP clerk. An $8,400 invoice from a new vendor landed on your desk — your supervisor needs an approval email before payment. Fill in all five, then paste into any AI:

Role: ________________________________ Context: _____________________________ Task: ________________________________ Format: ______________________________ Constraints: _________________________
Discuss: Which of the 5 parts was hardest? (Most people freeze at Context.)
TWO techniques. Zero-shot: just ask. "Categorize this transaction: Staples $42.18" → the AI guesses. Few-shot: give it 3 examples first. "Staples → Office Supplies. Uber → Travel. Zoom → Software." Now ask about the new one — accuracy jumps dramatically because you've shown it YOUR categorization style. Few-shot is the cheat code for routine categorization tasks.
For ANY multi-step calculation or decision, add the magic words: "Think step by step before giving your final answer." Example: "A company has $120,000 in AR. $40,000 is over 90 days old. Historical uncollectible rate is 8%. Walk me through the allowance for doubtful accounts calculation step by step, then give the journal entry." Without CoT the AI often rushes to the wrong number. With CoT you see the math and can catch mistakes.
Quick decision tree. Simple lookup ("what's the formula for gross margin?") → zero-shot, just ask. Pattern matching ("categorize these 50 transactions like the examples") → few-shot. Multi-step reasoning (any calculation or decision with several variables) → chain-of-thought. Complex + patterned? Combine few-shot + CoT. Photograph this slide too.
AI can read messy text — run-on paragraphs, handwritten notes, scanned PDFs. But just like humans, it works BETTER when information is organized. Organized desk vs messy desk: you can find things on both, but faster and more reliably on the organized one. Structure = clarity = better answers.
Markdown is simple formatting you can type in any chat box. Three symbols do 90% of the work: # for headings, **double-asterisk** for bold, and - (hyphen) for bullet points. That's it. When you paste a policy, a report, or a memo into an AI chat, slap some headings and bullets on it first. You'll be shocked how much sharper the answers get.
Two other structured formats you'll HEAR about but never need to write. JSON — curly braces and colons, used by web apps. XML — angle-bracket tags, used by government filings and healthcare. If someone on your team says "the AI returns JSON," now you know: it's structured, key-value data. That's all you need.

Two different skills, both matter. Prompt Engineering = better INSTRUCTIONS. Context Engineering = better INFORMATION. An excellent prompt with zero context still can't tell you YOUR firm's vacation policy. Great context with a weak prompt still produces mushy answers. You need both. The next section is about the context half.
Screenshot demo. Left: ask Gemini "what's the depreciation policy at Smith & Co CPAs?" — cold. Answer: generic textbook statement, no specifics, maybe a caveat. Right: same question AFTER uploading the firm handbook. Answer: "Per page 14 of the handbook, Smith & Co uses straight-line depreciation over 5 years for computer equipment..." That's the jump. That's context engineering.
Three layers that together form an AI's context: (1) The PROMPT — your immediate question. (2) DOCUMENTS — reference materials you attach: policies, reports, data, templates. (3) INSTRUCTIONS — system-level guidance on how to behave ("always cite your source," "you're an AP clerk at Smith & Co"). Stack all three and the AI becomes a real assistant, not just a chatbot.
The concept you'll hear over and over: RAG = Retrieval-Augmented Generation. CLOSED BOOK (old AI): "I only know what I studied years ago." Limited to memorized training data, knowledge cutoff dates, hallucinations when unsure. OPEN BOOK (RAG): "Let me look that up in your documents." Uses YOUR data, always current, cites sources. RAG turns the AI from a "know-it-all" into a "know-where-to-look" system.
Remember embeddings from earlier? Same math, different job. When you upload your employee handbook, the AI breaks it into chunks and embeds each chunk into the vector space. When you ask "what's our PTO policy?" your QUESTION also gets embedded. The system finds the chunks whose vectors are closest to your question's vector. That's semantic retrieval. That's the R in RAG.
Where are those embeddings stored? A vector database. Products you might hear about: Pinecone, Weaviate, Chroma. Business analogy: a regular filing cabinet finds files by LABEL. A vector database finds files by WHAT THEY'RE ABOUT — even if the labels are different. You won't build one. You'll use software that has one built in (like NotebookLM, or Gemini's uploaded files, or a corporate AI tool).
Four reasons this matters specifically for accounting. (1) CITATIONS — ask for the page number, defend your answer. Huge for audit and tax. (2) CURRENT — your firm's policies are always up-to-date because they ARE the document. No "knowledge cutoff." (3) PRIVATE DATA — your clients, your policies, your numbers — not in the public model's training data. (4) AUDIT TRAIL — you can trace every answer back to a source, critical for professional liability and for explaining AI use to partners or regulators.

Three takeaways. (1) AI predicts, it doesn't know — always verify. Especially numbers, names, and citations. (2) RCTFC + lower temperature + ask for citations = 80% fewer bad answers with almost zero extra effort. (3) Garbage in, garbage out — context is the upgrade. Upload the real document; don't make the AI guess. That's it. Five minutes of prep beats an hour of cleanup.
